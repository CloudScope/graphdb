# Lab 5 — Answers & Explanations

**1. Indexed vs. unindexed anchor.** The `id` query's plan starts with `NodeIndexSeek`, going straight to the 1 matching node. The `openedDate` query's plan starts with `NodeByLabelScan` — it has to pull all 15 `Account` nodes (the 6 from Lab 4, `ACC-MERCHANT`, and 8 `ACC-NOISE-*`) through a `Filter` step before landing on the 1 that matches. Same logical result, very different amount of work — this is Lesson 5's claim about anchoring made visible: the index turns "check every node with this label" into "go directly to the one you want."

**2.** `dev-0003: 10`, `dev-0001: 2`, `dev-0002: 1`, `dev-0004: 1` (the last two may print in either order). `dev-0003` is now clearly a super-node relative to the other three devices, entirely because of the 8 noise transactions added in `setup.cypher` — none of which have anything to do with the fraud ring.

**3.** The targeted query returns 9 rows: `txn-6` plus all 8 noise transactions — everything else that happens to share `dev-0003` with the one known fraud transaction. The broad query returns 92 rows: `dev-0001` contributes 2 (`t1`↔`t3`, counted both directions), and `dev-0003` alone contributes 10 × 9 = 90 (every transaction on that device paired against every other one). Both queries answer a version of "what shares a device with what," but the targeted one starts from 1 anchor (via the `isFraud` index) while the broad one starts from all 14 `Transaction` nodes and lets the pairing at the super-node multiply out — exactly the "unconstrained traversal that happens to route through the busy node" case Lesson 5 warns about. Notice the 90 figure is `dev-0003`'s degree squared, roughly — that quadratic blow-up is what "cost proportional to degree" turns into the moment you stop anchoring and start pairing.

**4.** Both return the same path — `ACC-1004 → ACC-1005 → ACC-1006 → ACC-1001`, `edgeHops: 6` — because the actual distance (6) fits inside the `*1..6` bound, and `shortestPath()` already does a fairly efficient bidirectional search regardless of the upper bound. On a graph this small, don't expect a dramatic `DB Hits` gap between the two — the honest lesson here isn't "bounding always saves you visible work," it's that an unbounded `*` gives the planner no ceiling to work with at all, so as a graph grows (more accounts, longer legitimate chains, more branching at nodes like `ACC-MERCHANT`), the unbounded version's search frontier has no cap while the bounded one does. The gap that matters shows up at scale, not on a 30-node toy graph — which is itself worth internalizing: some of these costs are invisible until the data is big enough to expose them.

**5.** `NodeIndexSeek` on `Transaction(isFraud)` runs first, landing on the single `isFraud: true` row, and only then expands to `Device` — the `isFraud` index (added in `setup.cypher`) makes the transaction side far more selective (1 match) than starting from `Device` would be. This is the planner making the same choice Lesson 5 recommends making by hand: anchor on the selective, indexed side.

## Lesson 5 check-your-understanding — answers

**1. What does an unindexed `Account.id` lookup cost, and why is that the same cost class as a relational table scan?**

Without an index, `MATCH (a:Account {id: "ACC-1001"})` compiles to `NodeByLabelScan` followed by a `Filter` — Neo4j has to pull every node labeled `Account` (15 of them here) and check each one's `id` property, exactly as exercise 1 showed. That's `O(n)` in the number of `Account` nodes, the identical cost shape as `SELECT * FROM accounts WHERE id = 'ACC-1001'` with no index on `accounts.id` in a relational database: both engines are doing "read every row/node of this kind and check a property," because neither has a shortcut to the specific one you want. Index-free adjacency never claimed otherwise — it's a statement about the *next* step (following `SENT`, `OWNS`, etc. from a node you're already standing on), not about how you find your first node.

**2. Why did the fraud query in Lab 4 stay cheap despite `Device` being a super-node, while a `Device`-first query wouldn't?**

Exercise 3 makes this concrete: anchoring on `Transaction {isFraud: true}` starts from exactly 1 node (via the `isFraud` index), takes one hop to its device, then one hop out to that device's other transactions — the resulting work is proportional to `dev-0003`'s degree (10), which is unavoidable if you actually want to know what shares that device, but it's *only* that, nothing more. A query that instead started from every `Transaction` (or every `Device`) and tried to compute the same "what shares a device with what" relationship for the whole graph has to pay that degree-proportional cost *at every anchor*, and at a device like `dev-0003` the pairing multiplies out — 90 rows from one device alone, versus the 9 that were actually relevant to the fraud case. Same super-node, same underlying cost-per-hop rule from Lesson 5; the difference is entirely in how many places in the graph you make that hop from.
